A small business website costs **$50 to $500 a month** to maintain. That is the honest headline number, and it is the one most articles make you scroll three times to find.

Here is the full range, since it is genuinely wide. A personal blog runs $5 to $75 a month. A small business site runs $50 to $500. An e-commerce store runs $300 to $1,000. Enterprise sites go past $5,000. The extreme upper end of the industry sits around $25,000 a month.

If you are a small business owner, the only row that matters is the second one. Everything else on this page explains where inside that range you land.

(Yes, I am aware I just gave away the answer in the first 60 words and then wrote 2,000 more. This is a blog post. It has a format. We are all doing our part.)

## The short version

Most small business sites should budget $50 to $500 a month. My own plans are $99, $199 and $399. The price is driven by four things: what the site does, how much it changes, how much traffic it gets, and whether anyone is actually watching it.

## What website maintenance costs, by site type

These are industry ranges, not my prices. I have put my own numbers in the next section so you can compare.

| Site type | Monthly | Yearly | What drives it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal blog | $5–$75 | $60–$900 | Mostly automated; low stakes |
| Small business site | $50–$500 | $600–$6,000 | Content changes, enquiries, uptime |
| E-commerce store | $300–$1,000 | $3,600–$12,000 | Payment security, stock, order integrity |
| Corporate or custom build | $1,000–$5,000+ | $12,000–$60,000+ | Integrations, compliance, traffic |
| Enterprise | $2,000–$25,000+ | $24,000–$300,000+ | Scale, SLAs, dedicated teams |

Two things worth noticing.

First, the jump from blog to small business site is roughly tenfold. That is not because the work is ten times harder. It is because the consequences are ten times worse. A broken blog is an annoyance. A broken enquiry form is lost revenue.

Second, the range within each row is enormous. A $50 site and a $500 site can both be described as "small business." The difference is what you are actually buying.

## What you are buying at each price point

Under $50 a month, you are buying automation. Automated backups, automatic plugin updates, maybe a security plugin. Nobody looks at your site. That is not necessarily bad — for a hobby blog it is completely sufficient. Just do not call it maintenance.

**$100 to $250 a month** is where professional maintenance starts. Someone logs in, runs the updates, checks the site still works afterwards, confirms the backup completed, and fixes what broke. That last part is the whole service. Updates that break things are not rare.

**$250 to $500 a month** adds content work, SEO monitoring, performance tuning and a real person you can email when something looks wrong.

**Past $500**, you are usually buying availability commitments, staging environments for testing changes, and priority response times. For most small businesses this is more than you need.

## What drives your price up

Four things. You control three of them.

**Plugin count.** Every plugin is another thing that can break during an update. I have inherited sites running 47 plugins. Those sites cost more to maintain because every update cycle is a small risk assessment.

**How often the site changes.** A site that gets new content weekly needs someone paying attention weekly. A site frozen since 2023 does not.

**Traffic and revenue at stake.** The more your site earns, the more an hour of downtime costs you. Maintenance pricing reflects that exposure.

**Whether the site was built sensibly.** This is the one you cannot fix cheaply. A site built on abandoned plugins and a hacked-together theme costs more to maintain forever. [Rebuilding it](/services/hire-wordpress-developer/) is often cheaper than maintaining it.

## What it costs me to maintain a site

Worth saying, because pricing gets opaque in this industry.

Routine monthly maintenance on a typical small business site is under two hours of work. So when you pay $199 a month, you are not paying for two hours of labour. You are paying for:

- Those two hours, every month, guaranteed
- The months where nothing goes wrong
- The month where a plugin update takes the site down and it gets fixed before you notice
- Me knowing your site well enough to fix it quickly instead of billing you to learn it again

That is the actual product. It is closer to insurance than to a repair service, and it should be priced like insurance.

**One strong opinion while I am here.** Any maintenance provider charging a year upfront for a $99 service is not confident in their own renewal rate. Maintenance is a monthly service. Price it monthly. If the work is good, people stay.

## What happens if you skip it

Three real outcomes, roughly in order of how often I see them.

A **plugin conflict** takes the site down. Usually after an automatic update at night. You find out from a customer.

An **outdated plugin gets exploited**. The site stays up but starts redirecting visitors somewhere unpleasant. Google notices before you do and flags the site. Getting that warning lifted takes days after the cleanup finishes.

A **form quietly stops working**. Nobody notices for weeks. You assume enquiries are down because of the market.

Malware cleanup typically runs $350 to $800 depending on depth. A blacklisting adds days of lost traffic on top. Compare that to $1,188 for a year of Basic Care.

## How to spend less

Four legitimate ways, in order of how much they save.

**Cut your plugins.** Every plugin removed is one less thing to update, secure and troubleshoot. Sites I have stripped from 40 plugins down to 12 became cheaper to maintain immediately.

**Check what your host already includes.** Many hosts now include daily backups and security scanning. If you are paying separately for both, you may be paying twice.

**Match the plan to the site.** A brochure site does not need a $399 plan. If your provider has not asked what your site actually does, they are selling a tier, not a service.

**Drop the extras.** SEO monitoring on a site with no SEO strategy is a line item, not a service. Plenty of small businesses pay for reporting nobody reads.

## Before you buy anything

- Ask for the task list in writing. Not "peace of mind" — actual tasks.
- Ask who does the work and whether you can talk to them.
- Ask what happens at 9pm on a Friday when the site is down.
- Ask for the notice period. It should be 30 days, not 12 months.
- Ask to see a recent backup. If they hesitate, that is your answer.
